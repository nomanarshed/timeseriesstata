Time series analysis sits at the centre of applied macroeconomics, finance, and energy economics. Whenever a single entity is observed over time — a country's GDP, an exchange rate, a commodity price, a firm's returns — the time dimension brings its own set of problems: trends, unit roots, autocorrelation, seasonality, structural breaks, and changing volatility. Stata handles all of it, but the commands are spread across a wide methodological range, from simple regression to ARDL, causality testing, error correction, and GARCH.

This playlist organises the workflow into a sensible learning path. Work through the stages in order. Each one assumes the previous: you cannot interpret an ARDL bounds test without first getting the series imported and stationarity sorted, and the logic of error correction only makes sense once the regression and cointegration foundations are in place.

Stages 1–4 form the core applied workflow, from data import through volatility and projection. Stages 5 and 6 are advanced extensions layered on that foundation — Fourier and quantile cointegration, mixed-order systems, and a specialised toolkit of quantile-on-quantile, wavelet, and visualisation methods — for researchers pushing beyond the standard path.

Stage 1

Foundations & Stationarity

Every time series analysis is only as reliable as the series behind it and the stationarity checks applied to it. Before any model runs, the data must be brought in cleanly, declared to Stata as time series, transformed appropriately, and tested for unit roots. This stage covers the unglamorous but decisive groundwork — getting data out of Excel, deciding between levels and logs, repairing gaps, and establishing the integration order of each series with modern, break- and quantile-robust unit-root tests.

Stage 2

Regression & ARDL Models

With clean, stationarity-checked data in hand, this stage builds the core modelling toolkit. It starts with simple time series regression and the diagnostics that must accompany it, then moves through the ARDL family — the workhorse for series mixing I(0) and I(1) variables — into its asymmetric, threshold, and quantile variants for nonlinear and distribution-dependent effects.

Stage 1 leaves you with a properly declared, gap-free series of known integration order. From here, the question becomes how variables relate over time. ARDL is the natural starting framework because it tolerates mixed orders of integration and recovers both short-run and long-run effects in one specification — which is why so much applied time series work lives here.

Stage 3

Causality & Dynamics

Estimating a relationship is not the same as understanding its direction or its dynamics over time. This stage covers the tools that go beyond a single fitted equation — formal causality testing robust to integration order, rolling-window estimation that reveals how relationships evolve, the error correction framework that ties short-run movements to a long-run equilibrium, and asymmetric causality that separates the influence of positive and negative shocks.

Stage 4

Volatility & Projection

This stage moves from modelling the mean of a series to modelling its variance, and then to communicating results. Financial and high-frequency data routinely show volatility clustering that the mean models in earlier stages assume away. It closes the core path with conditional volatility modelling and a tool for turning estimated relationships into clear, projectable visuals.

Everything up to here has modelled the conditional mean — what a series is expected to do on average. But in finance and energy data, the interesting action is often in the variance: quiet periods punctuated by bursts of turbulence. ARCH and GARCH models exist precisely to capture that, and they are the natural endpoint once the mean dynamics are well understood.

Stage 5

Advanced Cointegration

The standard ARDL and Engle-Granger machinery assumes a fairly clean world: no structural breaks, a single conditional mean, and variables that are at most I(1). Real macro and financial systems frequently violate all three. This stage gathers the advanced cointegration tools for those harder cases — smooth breaks via Fourier terms, distribution-dependent equilibria via quantile cointegration, and systems mixing I(0), I(1), and I(2) variables.

Stages 1–4 give you the standard applied path. The methods here are extensions, not prerequisites — reach for them when your data resists the usual assumptions: a long-run relationship that only holds in certain quantiles, an equilibrium that drifts with structural change, or a variable that is genuinely I(2).

Stage 6

Specialised Methods & Visualisation

The final stage collects powerful methods that sit outside the linear-model mainstream, together with the visualisation tools that make complex results legible. Quantile-on-quantile regression and wavelet analysis expose structure that mean-based models cannot see; nomographs and a disciplined model-selection workflow turn that structure into something a reader — or a reviewer — can actually use.

These are the specialised instruments you reach for once the core workflow is second nature: when you need to see how a whole distribution responds to another distribution, how a relationship lives across time and frequency at once, or how to defend your final model choice with evidence rather than habit.

A note on sequencing

Stage 1 is a prerequisite, not a suggestion. If your series is not imported and declared correctly with tsset, the lag and difference operators that every later command relies on will either fail or silently mislead. If you have not settled the levels-versus-logs and stationarity questions, an ARDL bounds test in Stage 2 cannot be interpreted.

Stages 3 and 4 carry more flexibility depending on your research focus. If your interest is the direction of influence between variables, the causality and ECM videos are the core. If you work with financial or high-frequency data, the ARCH–GARCH material is where the real questions live. Stages 5 and 6 are advanced extensions — reach for Fourier or quantile cointegration, mixed-order systems, quantile-on-quantile regression, or wavelets only once the foundation beneath them is solid. The stronger that foundation before specialising, the better your instincts will be when something goes wrong — and in time series, the trouble usually hides in the data preparation you were tempted to rush.

These methods are genuinely hard to use well. The places where they break — a missed unit root, an ignored break, an autocorrelated residual — are rarely obvious until a reviewer asks the question you should have asked yourself.
